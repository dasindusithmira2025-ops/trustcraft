# TrustCraft — DHACK Grand Final Speaker Script

**Deck:** `output/TrustCraft_DHACK_Grand_Final.pptx` · 20 slides · 12 chapters
**Target spoken time: 9:30** (limit 10:00, so ~30 s of margin)
**Pace assumption:** ~145 words per minute. Slower than you think you talk.

Timings below are for the **spoken** part only. The 5-minute prototype demo and
the 5-minute Q&A are separate and are not counted here.

**How to read this**
- `[CLICK]` — advance the slide. Morph does the animating; you do not wait for it.
- `[HOLD]` — stop talking. Silence is doing work.
- `[STAGE]` — where to stand, what to point at.
- `[CUT IF BEHIND]` — the first thing to drop. Dropping all of them saves ~40 s.

Do not read the slides. Everything on a slide is there so the audience can
follow while you say something slightly different.

## 01 · Cold open — 0:30 (slides 1–2) · running 0:30

**[STAGE] Centre. Do not touch the clicker for the first eight seconds.**

> Something breaks at your house. A tap, a socket, the AC.
>
> Finding *someone* is easy. There are numbers everywhere.

**[CLICK] — slide 2. The problem types itself into the app.**

> Trusting them is the hard part.
>
> This is TrustCraft. It makes finding a home-service professional you can
> actually trust as simple as describing what went wrong — in your own words.

*(~68 words)*

## 02 · The real problem — 0:50 (slides 3–4) · running 1:20

**[STAGE] Step left. Let the scatter sit on screen for two seconds before you speak.**

> This is what people actually do today.
>
> Ask a friend. Search a Facebook group. Google it. Call three numbers.
> Send the same photos on WhatsApp, explain the same thing again, compare
> prices you have no way of judging — and then guess.

**[CLICK] — slide 4. The scatter collapses into three columns.**

> Underneath all of that there are only three problems.
>
> **Discovery** — where do I even find someone.
> **Evaluation** — can I trust this person in my house.
> **Communication** — how do I explain what is wrong when I do not know the
> name of the thing that is broken.
>
> Too much friction. Too little trust.

**[CUT IF BEHIND]** the whole "ask a friend / Facebook / Google" list — just say
"people run a five-step scavenger hunt and then guess."

*(~118 words)*

---

## 03 · Two sides — 0:40 (slide 5) · running 2:00

**[STAGE] Point left, then right. Do not read the small caps.**

> This gap has two sides, and both are real.
>
> On the left, the homeowner: *"I need help quickly — but who can I trust?"*
>
> On the right, an actual skilled professional: *"I'm good at my work. How do
> the right customers find me?"*
>
> Skill is not their problem. Proving it outside their own network is.
>
> Both sides are missing the same thing. Proof. And these are both live screens
> from our build — one product, two apps.

*(~92 words)*

---

## 04 · The reveal — 0:45 (slides 6–7) · running 2:45

> So we built TrustCraft around one idea: **describe the problem, and let the
> platform do the searching.**
>
> You should not have to know whether a leaking sink is a plumbing job, a
> fitting job or a carpentry job. That is our work, not yours.

**[CLICK] — slide 7. One screen becomes three.**

> Three phases. **Describe** — write it, photograph it, or say it.
> **Understand** — TrustCraft structures the problem and works out the trade.
> **Discover** — a short, ranked list of verified professionals, with the
> reason for the ranking shown.
>
> Not a directory. A decision.

*(~106 words)*

## 05 · The peak — 1:10 (slides 8–10) · running 3:55

**[STAGE] This is the moment. Slow down. Move to the screen.**

> People do not describe a broken thing the same way twice, so we accept three
> kinds of input.
>
> **Typed** — *"My AC turns on but the room doesn't get cold."*
> **Shown** — point the camera at the fault.
> **Spoken** — *"My bathroom tap has been leaking since yesterday."*

**[CLICK] — slide 9. The three converge.**

> All three land in the same place: one structured problem — plumbing repair,
> a leak, kitchen sink, Colombo 05 — and three verified professionals, ranked.
>
> The customer describes a symptom. TrustCraft returns a decision.

**[CLICK] — slide 10. Two clips loop.**

> And this is not a mockup.

**[HOLD — 3 seconds. Let the clips run. Say nothing.]**

> That is the running build. You type what is wrong; it becomes a category and
> a shortlist.
>
> You don't need to know the service. Just tell us what's wrong.

*(~163 words)*

## 06 · Trust is the product — 1:00 (slides 11–12) · running 4:55

**[STAGE] Point at the trust score. Do not read the annotations.**

> Now the part that actually matters.
>
> Finding three names is easy. Knowing which one to let into your house is not.
>
> So the profile is built to answer that. A **Trust Score out of 100** — not a
> star average. Underneath it, the evidence: verified identity, years on the
> tools, works completed, distance, the inspection fee, availability. And
> reviews that are attached to jobs the platform actually tracked.
>
> The transformation is: find someone → **choose confidently.**

**[CLICK] — slide 12.**

> And the score is earned, not claimed. The professional sees the same number,
> and exactly what moves it — identity, certification, on-time arrival,
> quotation accuracy, completion evidence.
>
> A rating tells you people were happy. A score tells you *why you should be.*

*(~142 words)*

## 07 · UX by design — 0:45 (slide 13) · running 5:40

**[STAGE] Point at marker 1, then 2, then 3. One sentence each. Do not lecture.**

> This is a UI/UX competition, so let me show you three decisions rather than
> list laws.
>
> **One.** Three ranked matches, not a directory of forty — Hick's Law. And the
> match percentage is visible, so the ranking can be argued with.
>
> **Two.** One full-width primary action per screen, in thumb reach, and
> nothing else on the screen is blue — Fitts and Von Restorff.
>
> **Three.** Text, photo, video or voice — Postel's Law. The interface adapts
> to the person, instead of handing them a form.
>
> Designed to minimise decisions, not maximise screens.

**[CUT IF BEHIND]** the aesthetic-usability line below.

> *(optional)* A calm interface is also read as a trustworthy one — which on a
> trust product is not decoration, it is function.

*(~112 words)*

## 08 · Market evidence — 0:50 (slide 14) · running 6:30

**[STAGE] Let the number land before you explain it.**

> One number.
>
> **Seventy-six per cent.** Sri Lanka has just over 1.1 million craft and
> related trades workers — plumbers, electricians, carpenters, masons. Three
> quarters of them work in the informal sector.
>
> That is the government's own definition: unregistered, no formal accounts,
> under ten employees. Which is precisely the state a customer cannot check.
>
> That is the trust gap, written as a statistic.
>
> And the channel is already there — 21.6 million mobile broadband
> subscriptions in a country of 21.9 million people.
>
> Skill is not scarce in Sri Lanka. Proof is.

**[CUT IF BEHIND]** the mobile broadband sentence.

*(~113 words)*

## 09 · Competitive position — 0:45 (slide 15) · running 7:15

**[STAGE] Be visibly fair here. Judges reward it.**

> We are not the first people to notice this.
>
> Servixy and TaskForce.lk both verify providers against NIC and hold payment
> in escrow. TaskForce mediates disputes. Blu vets profiles and leans on
> reviews. None of that is a weakness, and I am not going to pretend it is.
>
> The difference is the front door. Every one of them asks you to pick a
> category, or describe a job and name a budget, before anything happens. The
> person with water coming out of their kitchen cupboard knows neither.
>
> Existing platforms help you browse services. TrustCraft starts from the
> problem itself.

*(~110 words)*

---

## 10 · Business model — 0:45 (slide 16) · running 8:00

> The business model is already partly in the build.
>
> When a professional prices a job, the quotation tool shows them the payout
> before they send it. Customer pays 4,300. TrustCraft's fee is 8 per cent —
> 344 rupees. The professional receives 3,956.
>
> Everything on the right is **proposed** — not built, not earning. Search and
> trust evidence stay free for customers forever; charging the anxious side of
> a trust problem would break the product. Professionals get a free tier that
> is genuinely enough to work on, and a paid tier for business tools — which we
> will price only after real providers tell us what they would pay for.
>
> When providers grow, we grow. That is the whole alignment.

*(~124 words)*

---

## 11 · Scale — 0:45 (slide 17) · running 8:45

> Start narrow. Seven categories, Colombo — those are in the build today.
>
> Then more local-service categories, because the trust layer does not care
> what the trade is. Then beyond the household — property managers,
> maintenance networks, the people who need this proof at higher volume.
>
> And long term, the asset is not the marketplace. It is the **verified service
> record** — something a professional owns and carries, which today does not
> exist anywhere in this market.
>
> You will notice there are no dates, no user targets and no valuations on this
> slide. We would have to invent them. This is the order of the work.

*(~112 words)*
